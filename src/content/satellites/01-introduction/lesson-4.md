Satellites can be classified based on multiple factors listed below.

<div class="definitions-grid">
  <div class="definition-card">
    <h3>Scale</h3>
    <ul>
      <li><strong>Large satellites</strong> &mdash; many complex instruments on a single satellite. Only one satellite is available to collect data or transmit information.</li>
      <li><strong>Small satellites</strong> &mdash; many satellites working together to collect data or transmit information. These groups of satellites are referred to as artificial satellite constellations and can consist of a few satellites to thousands of satellites. These satellites together allow a short revisit time between satellite passes over a location.</li>
    </ul>
  </div>
<div class="definition-card">
    <h3>Orbital Altitude and Type</h3>
    <ul>
      <li><strong>Low earth orbit</strong> &mdash; Satellites are closer to Earth and typically have a polar or near-polar orbits. A satellite in a polar orbit pass directly over the north and south pole during each cycle. A satellite in a near-polar orbit follow a similar orbital pattern, however their orbit is slightly off-set from the north and south poles. The rotation is slightly offset, so the satellite will pass over all parts of the Earth over time. Satellites in these orbits will pass over the earth multiple times per day, visiting the same spot on the earth during the day and at night. The timing of the satellite passing over the equator occurs at the same time each day, putting the satellite in sun-synchronous orbit.</li>
      <li><strong>Medium earth orbit</strong> &mdash; Satellites are further distanced from Earth and orbit in a semi-synchronous or Molinia orbital pattern. A satellite in a Semi-synchronous orbit rotates in a near-polar orbit around the Earth at a speed that allows it to pass the equator at the same spot twice each day. GPS satellites use these types of orbits. Satellites in a Molniya orbit circle the Earth similarly to semi-synchronous orbits, however their orbit is off centered from the Earth, which causes the satellite to move at different speeds depending on their proximity to the Earth. When the satellite is closer to the Earth, it moves at a higher speed than when it is located at the far side of oval orbit. This orbit pattern is used for communication purposes near the poles.<sup>4</sup></li>
      <li><strong>High earth orbit</strong> &mdash; Satellites are at an altitude about 22370 miles above Earth's surface. Satellites at this altitude rotate at the same speed as Earth and are considered in geostationary orbit. This type of orbit is useful for weather satellites.<sup>4</sup></li>
    </ul>
  </div>
  <div class="definition-card">
    <h3>Ownership</h3>
    <ul>
      <li><strong>Government Satellites</strong> &mdash; NASA, ESA, or others that often allow data to be freely used by the public.</li>
      <li><strong>Commercial satellites</strong> &mdash; privately owned and operated satellites with a variety of purposes. Often require payment to use satellite data.</li>
    </ul>
  </div>
</div>
