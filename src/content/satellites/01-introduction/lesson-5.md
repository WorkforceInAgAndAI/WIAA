The position of the satellite above the Earth and the angles between the satellite, object of interest, and the sun all impact the quality of the image captured and the area of coverage. The line of sight is the direction of the sensor to the object of interest.

<p>Satellites positioned directly over the object of interest have their sensors positioned at nadir, or a 0&deg; viewing angle. If the sensor is intended to be positioned at nadir, but it tilts out from nadir, the sensor becomes positioned at an off-nadir angle. The off-nadir angle is a measure of the distortion from the nadir angle and is calculated by the angle created between the nadir angle and the off-nadir angle.</p>

<p>Sensors may be purposefully positioned at an angle different from the nadir angle. The angle created between the line of sight and nadir form the satellite is the look angle. The between the line of sight and a line directly vertical or nadir to the object of interest is the incidence angle. Sensors that are positioned nadir result in images with high clarity, however, sensors that are positioned at an angle away from nadir result in images capturing the sides of objects of interest, covering larger areas, and increased temporal resolution.</p>

<p>The angle of the sun with the object of interest also has an impact on the quality of the image by influencing the light, glare, and shadows interacting with the object of interest. The zenith is the representation of a vertical line upwards from the object of interest. The angle created between the direct path created by the sun and the object of interest, and the zenith is the zenith angle. The angle between the sun's path with the object and the horizon (or 90&deg; - zenith angle) is the elevation angle.</p>

<p>The side of the object of interest illuminated by the sun is represented by the azimuth angle, which is calculated as the angle between the true north and the direction of the sun from the target in a clockwise direction.</p>
